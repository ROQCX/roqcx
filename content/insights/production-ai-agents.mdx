# From AI Prototype to Production: How to Build Reliable AI Agents

Building an AI agent has become remarkably accessible.

With modern large language models, a developer can create an agent that interprets requests, calls external APIs and completes basic workflows with relatively little code.

The initial demonstration can be impressive.

An agent retrieves customer records, searches internal documentation, updates a ticket or produces a report in response to a natural-language request.

But there's an important distinction between a successful demonstration and a production application.

The demonstration shows that something is possible.

Production requires proving that it works reliably, securely and at an acceptable cost, including when things go wrong.

For organisations investing in AI agents, understanding that difference is essential.

## What is a production AI agent?

An AI agent is a software system that uses an AI model to interpret information, determine appropriate next steps and interact with tools or other systems to complete tasks.

Unlike a conventional chatbot, which primarily generates responses, an agent can perform actions.

For example, a customer service agent might retrieve an order, investigate a delivery issue and create a support ticket.

An internal operations agent might review incoming requests, classify them, retrieve supporting information and update a workflow system.

A finance assistant might extract information from invoices, identify discrepancies and prepare transactions for approval.

The technical foundations of these applications are often relatively simple.

Typically, they involve:

- A large language model
- Instructions defining its role and objectives
- Tools for accessing business systems
- Application logic managing execution and state
- Controls governing what the agent can do

The challenge isn't necessarily building these components.

It's ensuring they work together predictably under real operating conditions.

## Identify the right business process

Not every automation opportunity requires an AI agent.

This is an important consideration before selecting models, frameworks or technology platforms.

Many operational workflows are already well suited to conventional automation.

If the process follows a predictable set of rules, a scheduled job, workflow engine or traditional API integration may be simpler and less expensive.

AI becomes particularly useful when a process involves unstructured information, ambiguous requests or decisions that previously required a person to interpret context.

Consider three examples.

**Invoice reminders**

Sending a reminder seven days after an invoice becomes overdue is a straightforward rules-based workflow. It doesn't require an AI agent.

**Invoice dispute handling**

Interpreting a customer's explanation, identifying the relevant invoice and determining the appropriate next step may benefit from AI.

**Invoice adjustment**

Actually changing a financial record should remain subject to defined business rules, permissions and approval requirements.

The most effective solution may combine all three approaches.

AI interprets the request. Conventional software executes predictable steps. Business controls determine which actions are permitted.

This is often a better architecture than attempting to make an entire process autonomous.

## Define a narrow scope for the first agent

One of the most common implementation mistakes is attempting to automate an entire business function from the outset.

A more effective approach is to begin with a clearly defined workflow.

For example, rather than building an AI customer service agent capable of handling every request, start with an agent that can investigate order delivery enquiries.

Its responsibilities might include:

1. Identifying the customer's request.
2. Retrieving relevant orders.
3. Checking delivery information.
4. Explaining the current status.
5. Escalating unresolved issues.

This scope provides a measurable outcome without requiring access to every customer service capability.

It also makes testing easier.

The project team can evaluate whether the agent identifies the correct order, retrieves accurate information, responds appropriately and escalates when necessary.

Only once the workflow performs consistently should additional capabilities be introduced.

## Design the architecture around controlled tool access

An AI agent doesn't need unrestricted access to business applications.

Instead, it should interact with narrowly defined functions that expose specific capabilities.

Consider an agent connected to a CRM platform.

Rather than giving it broad access to the CRM API, the application could provide functions such as:

- `getCustomer(customerId)`
- `getOpenCases(customerId)`
- `createCase(customerId, issue)`

Each function should enforce authentication, authorisation and input validation independently of the model.

The agent can decide which function to request, but the application remains responsible for determining whether execution is permitted.

### A simple reference architecture

A practical implementation may include five layers.

**1. Interaction layer**

The interface through which users submit requests, such as a website, internal application, messaging platform or customer service channel.

**2. Agent orchestration layer**

The component responsible for managing model interactions, application state, tool requests, execution limits and workflow progression.

**3. Model layer**

The LLM that interprets requests, processes relevant context and determines the next appropriate step.

**4. Tools and integration layer**

Controlled functions connecting the agent to databases, business applications, search services and external APIs.

**5. Governance and execution layer**

The application services responsible for permissions, business rules, approvals, transaction integrity and execution logging.

These layers don't necessarily require separate infrastructure or services.

For smaller deployments, several may exist within the same application.

The purpose of the architecture is to separate responsibilities, not to introduce complexity for its own sake.

## Keep orchestration as simple as possible

Multi-agent architectures are increasingly common, but they aren't always necessary.

A single AI agent with a carefully designed set of tools can perform many useful business workflows.

Adding additional agents may make sense when tasks require distinct expertise, isolated permissions or independent processing.

For example, an enterprise research workflow might benefit from separate components for retrieving information, analysing data and preparing a report.

However, distributing work across multiple agents can also introduce additional latency, cost and operational complexity.

Each model interaction creates another opportunity for incorrect interpretation or inconsistent outputs.

Before introducing multiple agents, assess whether the requirement could be met through:

- A conventional application function
- A deterministic workflow
- Improved tool definitions
- Better context management
- A single agent with clearly defined responsibilities

The objective should be the simplest architecture that satisfies the business and operational requirements.

## Implement security and governance controls

Production AI agents require the same security fundamentals as other enterprise applications.

This includes authentication, authorisation, least-privilege access, secrets management, data protection and audit logging.

But agents also introduce additional considerations.

They may interpret untrusted information, select tools dynamically and propose actions based on incomplete or incorrect reasoning.

### Protect against prompt injection

An agent retrieving information from emails, websites or documents may encounter content containing malicious instructions.

For example, a customer email could include instructions attempting to make the agent reveal confidential information or ignore its operating rules.

External content should therefore be treated as untrusted data, not as an authoritative instruction source.

### Enforce business rules outside the model

An agent responsible for preparing financial transactions might be instructed not to exceed a particular amount.

That restriction should be enforced by the transaction service itself.

The model's interpretation of the instruction should not be the only safeguard.

### Control sensitive actions

Actions involving money, confidential information, destructive operations or significant customer impact should have appropriate controls.

Depending on risk, these may include additional validation, explicit user confirmation or independent human approval.

### Maintain a clear audit trail

Organisations should be able to identify which tools an agent requested, what checks were performed and which actions were actually executed.

Logging should be sufficient for troubleshooting and accountability while protecting sensitive data.

Security needs to be part of the application architecture from the beginning, rather than added after the agent has been developed. Our guide to [implementing AI governance](/insights/implementing-ai-governance) explains how to set these controls at the organisation level.

## Test the agent as a complete system

Traditional software testing remains important, but AI agents require additional evaluation techniques.

Because model behaviour is probabilistic, a small number of successful demonstrations cannot establish reliability.

Testing should cover both the quality of the agent's reasoning and the correctness of the surrounding application.

### Functional testing

Can the agent complete the intended task?

Does it retrieve the correct information, select appropriate tools and produce usable outcomes?

### Integration testing

What happens when an API is unavailable, a request times out or a connected application returns unexpected data?

Does the system recover safely, retry appropriately or escalate the issue?

### Security testing

Can the agent be manipulated into requesting unauthorised actions?

Can external content influence its behaviour inappropriately?

Can users access information belonging to other customers?

### Workflow evaluation

How does the agent perform across realistic variations of the same task?

For example, can it handle vague customer requests, missing order numbers, conflicting information and multiple open orders?

### Regression testing

Does the system continue to perform as expected after changes to the model, prompts, tools or underlying business applications?

These evaluations should become part of the ongoing delivery process.

They shouldn't be treated as a one-time activity before launch.

## Design for failure

One of the most important differences between prototypes and production systems is how they handle failure.

An agent may request the wrong tool, fail to retrieve information or generate an incorrect response.

A connected API may become unavailable.

A user may submit an unexpected request.

Production architecture needs to account for these scenarios.

Useful controls include:

**Execution limits:** Restrict the number of model interactions and tool calls allowed within a task.

**Timeouts:** Prevent workflows from running indefinitely.

**Retry policies:** Retry transient failures only where it is safe to do so.

**Idempotency:** Prevent duplicate actions when the same request is processed more than once.

**Fallback workflows:** Transfer unresolved tasks to conventional application processes or human operators.

**Recovery mechanisms:** Preserve enough state to investigate or safely resume interrupted workflows.

These controls may not be particularly exciting, but they're essential to operational reliability.

In many cases, they're also more important than selecting a more sophisticated AI model.

## Measure business outcomes, not just model performance

A production AI agent should deliver measurable value.

That means moving beyond demonstrations of what the model can do and assessing whether the overall solution improves business performance.

Consider a customer service agent.

Traditional AI evaluations might focus on response accuracy or tool-selection quality.

Those metrics matter, but the business will also want to understand:

| Metric | Business question |
| --- | --- |
| Successful resolution rate | How many enquiries are resolved correctly? |
| Human escalation rate | How often is additional support required? |
| Average resolution time | Is the process becoming faster? |
| Cost per resolved enquiry | Is the solution commercially sustainable? |
| Incorrect action rate | How often does the system make operational mistakes? |
| Customer satisfaction | Is the experience actually improving? |

These measurements should be compared against the existing process.

An agent that reduces handling time but increases customer complaints may not represent an improvement.

Similarly, an agent that successfully completes tasks but requires expensive model calls and frequent manual corrections may struggle to demonstrate a viable return on investment.

Product success needs to be measured at the workflow level, not simply at the model level.

## Introduce autonomy progressively

Organisations don't need to move immediately from manual workflows to autonomous agents.

A progressive deployment model can reduce risk and provide opportunities to validate performance.

**Stage 1: Assist**

The AI retrieves information and recommends actions, but a person remains responsible for execution.

**Stage 2: Prepare**

The AI prepares structured actions or updates for human approval.

**Stage 3: Execute within boundaries**

The AI can initiate approved, low-risk actions within predefined limits.

**Stage 4: Expand controlled autonomy**

The AI handles a broader range of workflows, with appropriate monitoring, policy enforcement and escalation.

Progression between these stages should depend on demonstrated performance and risk tolerance rather than a fixed deployment timeline.

In some workflows, human approval should remain a permanent requirement.

The objective isn't maximum autonomy.

It's the appropriate level of autonomy for the business outcome and associated risk.

## Establish an operating model

Once an AI agent is deployed, someone needs to own its performance.

Business requirements will change, integrations will evolve and the application may require updates to its models, prompts or tools.

Organisations should establish clear responsibilities for:

- Monitoring operational performance
- Reviewing incidents and failed tasks
- Maintaining integrations and permissions
- Managing model and application changes
- Evaluating cost and performance
- Updating instructions and business rules
- Retesting critical workflows before deployment

A production AI agent should be treated as an actively managed software product, not a completed automation project.

This is especially important for applications embedded in customer-facing or business-critical processes.

## Moving beyond the prototype

Building an AI prototype has never been easier.

That presents a significant opportunity for organisations to experiment, validate ideas and identify potential improvements to existing processes.

But moving into production requires a different level of discipline.

It requires clear business ownership, thoughtful architecture, controlled integrations, appropriate governance and a mechanism for continuously evaluating performance.

The organisations that benefit most from AI agents won't necessarily be the ones deploying the most sophisticated architectures.

They'll be the ones that understand where AI adds value and can translate that capability into dependable business processes.
