# How to Implement AI Governance Without Slowing Down Innovation

AI adoption is moving quickly. In many organisations, employees are already using AI tools to summarise documents, analyse information, draft communications and improve everyday workflows.

Meanwhile, technology teams are introducing more sophisticated AI capabilities, including agents that can retrieve information, interact with business applications and execute tasks.

The question is no longer whether organisations should adopt AI. For many, adoption is already happening.

The more pressing question is how to introduce appropriate controls without creating so much bureaucracy that teams stop innovating.

This is where a practical approach to AI governance becomes important.

The objective shouldn't be to restrict AI unnecessarily. It should be to establish clear boundaries that allow organisations to adopt AI confidently, understand the associated risks and retain control over business-critical decisions.

## What is AI governance?

AI governance refers to the policies, processes, responsibilities and technical controls used to manage how artificial intelligence is developed, deployed and used within an organisation.

It addresses several important questions:

- What AI tools and applications are permitted?
- What information can employees share with AI systems?
- Who is accountable for AI-generated decisions and actions?
- How do we evaluate the reliability and security of AI applications?
- What controls are required before an AI system can take action?
- How do we monitor AI systems once they're in production?

Effective governance needs to address both the organisational and technical aspects of AI adoption.

A responsible AI policy is useful, but a policy alone doesn't prevent an application from accessing sensitive information or executing an unauthorised transaction.

Those protections must also exist within the systems themselves.

## Start with understanding how AI is already being used

One of the first mistakes organisations make is developing an AI policy before understanding how employees are actually using the technology.

A better starting point is an AI usage assessment.

This doesn't have to become a lengthy consulting exercise.

The initial objective is to understand which tools are being used, what information employees are sharing and which business processes are already dependent on AI.

Consider three areas.

### 1. Employee AI usage

Identify the AI tools employees use for everyday work.

This may include general-purpose assistants, AI features embedded in productivity tools, coding assistants and industry-specific applications.

Pay particular attention to whether employees use personal accounts, what information they upload and how generated outputs are reviewed.

### 2. AI embedded in business applications

Many organisations already use AI without necessarily treating it as a separate technology initiative.

Customer service platforms, CRM systems, marketing tools and enterprise applications increasingly include AI-powered functionality.

These capabilities should be considered within the overall governance approach, particularly where they process sensitive information or influence business decisions.

### 3. Custom AI applications and agents

Identify systems being developed internally or by technology partners.

These generally require more detailed technical oversight, particularly where they connect to internal databases, enterprise applications or transactional systems.

**The outcome should be a practical AI inventory**, identifying the applications in use, business owners, data accessed, associated risks and existing controls.

Without this visibility, governance is largely based on assumptions.

## Introduce risk-based governance

Not every use of AI creates the same level of risk.

Using an AI assistant to improve the wording of an internal email is fundamentally different from allowing an AI agent to modify employee records or process financial transactions.

A sensible governance framework should reflect those differences.

| Risk level | Example | Recommended controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Drafting non-sensitive content | Approved tools and basic usage guidelines |
| Moderate | Summarising internal documents | Access controls, data classification and output review |
| High | Customer-facing AI recommendations | Testing, monitoring, escalation and accountable ownership |
| Critical | Financial transactions or sensitive record changes | Enforced policy checks, independent authorisation and audit trails |

These are illustrative classifications. Actual risk should consider the industry, data involved, potential harm and regulatory obligations.

The benefit of this approach is that governance becomes proportionate.

Low-risk activities can proceed with relatively lightweight controls, while higher-risk applications require additional safeguards.

This avoids treating every AI use case as though it carries the same consequences.

## Move from AI policies to technical enforcement

One of the most important considerations for organisations introducing AI agents is the distinction between instructions and enforceable controls.

Imagine a customer service AI agent that can process refunds.

The organisation's policy states that refunds above AED 500 require manager approval.

We could include that instruction in the agent's system prompt.

But what prevents the agent from submitting a refund of AED 750 if it incorrectly interprets a customer's circumstances?

For business-critical actions, the answer shouldn't depend solely on whether the AI correctly understands the policy.

Instead, the refund service should independently verify the transaction amount and applicable approval requirements.

This principle applies to any AI system capable of taking consequential actions.

### A practical execution control model

Before executing an action, the application should verify:

**Evidence:** Is the information supporting the action accurate, current and sufficient?

**Business rules:** Does the action satisfy the system's non-negotiable constraints?

**Policy:** Is the proposed action permitted under applicable organisational policies?

**Authority:** Does the requesting user or agent have permission to perform it?

If these conditions are satisfied, the action can proceed.

If not, the system should deny the request or route it through an appropriate approval process.

The key architectural principle is that the agent shouldn't control its own authorisation.

These checks need to be enforced by a separate application or service, with no direct route around the controls.

This approach draws on established software engineering practices such as policy-as-code, role-based access control and transaction validation.

It is particularly relevant as AI moves beyond generating information and begins interacting directly with operational systems. Our guide to [building production-ready AI agents](/insights/production-ai-agents) covers how these controls fit into an agent's wider architecture.

## Establish clear accountability

AI governance cannot sit entirely with the IT department.

Different stakeholders have different responsibilities.

Business owners should be accountable for the outcomes of AI-enabled processes.

Technology teams should be responsible for application architecture, security and technical reliability.

Risk, compliance and legal teams should establish relevant policies and assess regulatory obligations.

Product teams should determine whether the AI capability solves a genuine customer or business problem, and how its performance will be evaluated.

Importantly, ownership should be explicit.

Every production AI application should have a named business owner responsible for its continued suitability, not just the team that originally developed it.

## Build monitoring and evaluation into the operating model

Governance doesn't end when an AI system goes live.

AI applications can behave differently as user behaviour changes, connected data evolves and underlying models are updated.

Organisations therefore need a mechanism for monitoring performance and identifying emerging risks.

For AI agents, useful measures include:

- Task completion and accuracy rates
- Failed or rejected actions
- Policy violations and attempted violations
- Human intervention and escalation rates
- Unexpected tool usage
- Security incidents and operational failures
- Changes in performance following application or model updates

These measures should be supported by appropriate logging, testing and incident management processes.

For high-impact applications, organisations should also be able to reconstruct consequential decisions from recorded inputs, tool activity, policy evaluations and execution outcomes.

Simply storing the final response generated by an LLM is unlikely to provide sufficient operational visibility.

## A practical roadmap for implementing AI governance

Organisations don't need to build an extensive governance function before they can start using AI effectively.

A phased approach is generally more useful.

### Phase 1: Assess

Create an inventory of AI tools and applications. Identify the information being processed, business owners, current controls and potential risks.

**Deliverable:** AI usage inventory and initial risk assessment.

### Phase 2: Define

Establish acceptable-use policies, data-handling requirements, risk classifications, responsibilities and approval processes.

Align these requirements with the organisation's existing security, privacy and risk management processes.

**Deliverable:** AI governance policy and operating model.

### Phase 3: Implement

Introduce technical controls where required.

These may include identity and access management, data protection, policy enforcement, transaction validation, human approvals and monitoring.

**Deliverable:** Enforceable controls for priority AI applications.

### Phase 4: Validate

Test how applications behave under normal, ambiguous and adverse conditions.

Confirm that unauthorised actions are prevented and that escalation procedures operate as intended.

**Deliverable:** Evaluation results and deployment readiness assessment.

### Phase 5: Improve

Monitor performance, review incidents and refine controls as business needs and AI capabilities evolve.

**Deliverable:** Ongoing governance reporting and improvement backlog.

The approach should be iterative. Organisations can begin with one business function or AI application, validate the controls and progressively expand coverage.

## Governance as a shared capability

As organisations deploy more AI applications, maintaining separate governance mechanisms for every system can become expensive and inconsistent.

A longer-term opportunity is to establish governance as a shared capability.

A central governance service could manage common policies, authority checks, evidence requirements and approval workflows across multiple AI-enabled applications.

Individual business systems would still enforce their own domain-specific rules, but they could use a common governance infrastructure for cross-cutting controls.

This is the principle behind **Governance-as-a-Service**.

The objective is not to centralise every business decision.

It is to make governance reusable, consistent and easier to maintain across an organisation's technology landscape.

For businesses anticipating broader adoption of AI agents, this can become an important architectural consideration.
